Title

The Eye

DrawnJ. Francis
Rev · DateV1 · Nov 2026
StatusIn development
A note on the look

The Eye's live map and controls borrow the look of a fighter jet's radar display and cockpit hardware. That styling is a theme I enjoy building in, not a selling point and not a statement about what the system is for. Underneath it is a general purpose sensor that can be set up almost anywhere you need to know where people are.

Project explanation

The Eye tracks a person moving through an ordinary 3D space and shows their position live on a screen: where they are, which way they are heading and how fast. What that information is used for is up to the application, whether that is documenting a process, keeping people clear of a hazard, or watching over a space for safety.

It is intended as a larger, more robust option than image-based tracking. Camera tracking is excellent for fine, close-up operations, but it needs light, a clear view and heavy processing. The Eye is aimed at the room-scale job of following people through a space, without any of those requirements.

Two kinds of sensor share the work. 24 GHz millimetre-wave radar modules (HLK-LD2450) watch the whole area and report the range, bearing and speed of up to three people, ten times a second. Once the radar has a target, an infrared time-of-flight rangefinder (Benewake TF-Luna) on a small pan and tilt turret is pointed at it and takes over the fine tracking. If the rangefinder loses the person, the radar hands them back. A laptop fuses both sensors into a single track and draws it on a live B-scope style map.

Applications

Nothing about the Eye is tied to one use. Some of the places it fits:

  • Residential: knowing where people are moving around a home
  • Home security and home defense: following a person through the house in the dark
  • Security: monitoring offices, facilities and restricted areas
  • Warehouses: tracking people around racking, forklifts and other moving equipment
  • Stores: understanding how people move through a retail floor
  • Laboratories: documenting procedures and keeping people clear of hazardous equipment
  • And many more: anywhere a live position of a person is more useful than a picture of them

The engineering problem

The Eye uses zero image-based cameras. It never captures a picture or video of anyone, and it works the same in complete darkness as in daylight.

The problem it sets out to solve is doing the jobs people normally give to image-recognition cameras, finding a person, following them, telling one person from another and estimating their height, without using that system at all. Radar and infrared ranging measure distance and motion directly, so the work shifts from recognising shapes in pixels to fusing a few precise measurements into a track that holds up.

How the numbers are made

Equations and assumptions

The panel solves for the steady flight path that balances thrust, lift, drag and weight. The stabilator sets the angle of attack through the pitching-moment balance; airspeed and climb angle are then the two unknowns. The thrust line is taken along the body axis.

Cm = Cm0 + Cmα·α + Cmδe·δe + Cmδf·δf = 0 (pitch trim) T·cos α − D − W·sin γ = 0 (along the path) L + T·sin α − W·cos γ = 0 (normal to the path) L = ½ρV²S·CL(α, δf, M) D = ½ρV²S·CD(CL, δf, δe, M) Ground speed = V·cos γ + wind

The solver scans airspeed for the root of (T cos α − D)² + (L + T sin α)² − W² and refines it by bisection. Steady flight has no acceleration, so extra thrust always becomes climb. If thrust exceeds weight, or the wing makes negative lift, there may be no steady solution, and the panel says so instead of guessing.

Valid range: subsonic (M ≤ 0.95), steady flight, attached flow. Stall and transonic conditions are flagged above the readouts.
